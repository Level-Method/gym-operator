Ten ways to make it
blow gym owners away

The architecture is right and the friction work is real. Every gap found points the same direction: the skill is excellent at knowing and disciplined at behaving — but thin on moments where the owner feels something happen. These ten add the feeling.

The review in brief

Holding up well

  • The skill/KB separation is clean and consistently enforced across all six files
  • Two-part onboarding with confirm-over-compose is genuinely low friction
  • Authority, privacy, and never-fake-a-save rules are airtight and repeated where they matter
  • Evergreen vs changing split will keep the KB healthy at month six, not just day one
  • The log-before-every-job habit is the real moat vs. a blank chat

The honest gaps

  • The interview advertises six kinds of hand-off work — the skill ships a procedure for one
  • Returning sessions start cold; nothing greets the owner with where things stand
  • Nothing tracks the goal across weeks, watches upcoming dates, or counts what got done
  • Grounding is invisible — the owner can't see that an answer came from THEIR pages
  • The first job stalls if the owner has no numbers handy; updates arrive silently

The ten recommendations

1

Ship the job library — five more jobs

Highest impact · closes the promise gap · ~5 one-page files

Onboarding asks "what would you hand off?" and offers examples — social posts, program promotion, member recognitions, lead follow-up, quiet-member check-ins. If the owner picks any of those, no procedure exists. The wow dies exactly where interest peaks.

Add five jobs in the weekly-review template: content-batch (a week of posts in their voice, from rhythm + voice + one prompt), member-recognitions (level-ups, milestones, birthdays → drafted shout-outs and certificates prep), lead-follow-up-prep (this week's inquiries → prioritized, drafted, never sent), quiet-members (attendance export → who's drifting, drafted check-ins for the owner to send), event-promo (a project folder → dated promo plan and drafts).

Whatever they name in onboarding, the answer is "yes — here's how I do that."

2

The welcome-back brief

Feels like magic · one new reference section

Sessions currently start cold. Define a return ritual: read goals, rhythm, the log, the latest review, and open work — then open with three lines: "Since last time: [what changed]. The fall challenge starts in 9 days — promo isn't drafted yet. Want your weekly review, or the promo first?"

This is the morning briefing from Part 3 of the webinar series, delivered today with zero automation — it just reads its own knowledge base well.

The owner opens a chat and it already knows what week it is.

3

The goal scoreboard

Keeps them coming back · one rule in weekly-review + brief

The weekly review reads the goal but never tracks the arc. Add one required line to every review and welcome-back brief: "Week 6 of 12 — you're at +7 of +12 members. On pace." Position, pace, and days remaining, every single time.

Nobody else in their life is keeping score on their goal. This does, every Monday.

4

The first-impression insight

The onboarding wow · one line in step 4

The step-4 recap plays back facts — proof of reading, but not of thinking. Require it to end with one non-obvious observation from the sources: "One thing I noticed: your website never mentions pricing, but your Instagram answers pricing questions in comments weekly." One, not three. Labeled as an observation, not advice.

Ten minutes in, it tells them something about their own gym they hadn't put together.

5

Show the receipts

Makes grounding visible · one output rule

The whole webinar proof is that grounded beats blank — but in daily use the grounding is invisible. End substantive outputs with one small line: Grounded in: goals · team · log #14. Skip it on trivial answers.

Trust compounds; hallucination fears shrink; and every output becomes a live demo of why the knowledge base matters. It also makes gaps obvious — a weak answer with a short receipts line tells the owner exactly which page needs feeding.

Every answer quietly proves it's THEIR operator, not a chatbot.

6

"Just talk your week at me" — minimum viable input for every job

Kills the first-run stall · one line per job spec

Step 7 runs a real job — but if the owner picked the weekly review and has no numbers handy, friction returns at the worst moment. Every job spec gets a minimum viable input: for the review, "talk your week at me for sixty seconds — voice note style. We'll do the numbers version next Monday."

There is never a reason the first run can't happen right now.

7

The voice guardrail on drafts

Protects the #1 complaint · one drafting rule

voice.md exists but nothing enforces it at the moment of drafting. Rule: before showing any member-facing draft, check it against voice.md's "never say" list and flag near-misses; offer two variants — on-voice, and one notch bolder — so corrections become choices instead of rewrites. Every correction feeds voice.md and the log.

The drafts stop sounding like AI within two sessions — and the owner watched it learn.

8

Role-aware operation

Makes the shared drive real · one section in operator-rules

The pitch says "your team can read it" — but nothing defines what happens when the coach or front-desk person is the one talking. Rule: if the person isn't the owner, check gym/team.md for what they own; work freely inside their lane, route everything else to the owner, and log who asked for what.

This also pre-builds the Part 3 webinar story — team delegation — into the skill before Part 3 exists.

The head coach opens it and it already knows what's theirs to run.

9

CHANGELOG + the update ritual

Turns updates into re-engagement · one file + one routing row

"Pull the latest" currently ends in silence. Ship CHANGELOG.md in the skill; after an update the operator reads it and says two lines: "New since your last update: a member-recognitions job. Want to try it on this month's level-ups?" — offering exactly one new capability, tied to their gym.

Every release becomes a reason for lapsed owners to come back, and a marketing beat for the repo.

The thing they installed in August is visibly better in October — and tells them so.

10

The wins ledger

Justifies the subscription · log tag + monthly surface

Nothing captures what the operator actually did for them. When the owner confirms something worked — review used, posts published, a quiet member re-engaged — log it tagged win. The monthly health check opens with the scoreboard: "This month: 4 weekly reviews, 11 posts drafted, 3 drifting members caught, the fall challenge promo shipped on time."

This is the answer to "is this worth $20 a month," written by the operator itself — and it's the testimonial engine for the webinar series and the community.

Once a month it shows them, in their own numbers, why they'd never turn it off.

The pattern across all ten: the skill already thinks like an operator. These make it feel like one.
